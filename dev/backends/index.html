Choosing a backend

The backends differ in accuracy, in cost and in the distributions they support. EADefault chooses among some of them automatically. The others, such as sampling and sigma points, are chosen explicitly.

using ExpectationApproximations, Distributions
import BayesBase
const EA = ExpectationApproximations
using SpecialFunctions: digamma
using Random: Xoshiro

All of them prepare a point set, do not allocate when evaluated, and pass dual numbers through the integrand.

Gauss–Hermite quadrature on a $d$-dimensional Gaussian is a tensor grid of $n^d$ points: with 20 points per dimension, that is 400 points in two dimensions and 8000 in three. The grid is capped at maxpoints, 10 000 by default: beyond it the rule has fewer points per dimension, and from nine dimensions it is the spherical-radial rule. The number of sigma points grows linearly in $d$, but sigma points are exact only up to degree 3 or 4.

The nodes of Gauss–Laguerre and Gauss–Jacobi quadrature, and the panels of the rule in unconstrained coordinates, depend on the shape of the Gamma or Beta, so ForwardDiff cannot differentiate through a shape parameter. It can differentiate through the scale of a Gamma.

Accuracy: an example

Take $x \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(2.5, 1)$, and then $\mathrm{Gamma}(0.3, 1)$, and two integrands with closed forms: the smooth $e^{-x}$, with $\mathbb{E}[e^{-x}] = 2^{-2.5}$, and $\log x$, with $\mathbb{E}[\log x] = \psi(2.5)$, which is singular at zero.

deviation(backend, f, q, exact) = abs(BayesBase.expectation(backend, f, q) - exact)
smooth(backend, a) = deviation(backend, x -> exp(-x), Gamma(a, 1.0), 2.0^-a)
singular(backend, a) = deviation(backend, log, Gamma(a, 1.0), digamma(a))
for a in (2.5, 0.3), (name, backend) in (
        "UnconstrainedLegendre()" => EA.UnconstrainedLegendre(),
        "GaussLaguerre(16)" => EA.GaussLaguerre(16),
        "GaussLaguerre(64)" => EA.GaussLaguerre(64),
        "QuantileLegendre(16)" => EA.QuantileLegendre(16),
        "QuantileLegendre(64)" => EA.QuantileLegendre(64),
        "MonteCarlo(10_000)" => EA.MonteCarlo(10_000, Xoshiro(1)),
    )
    println("shape ", a, "  ", rpad(name, 24), "smooth error ", round(smooth(backend, a); sigdigits = 2), "   singular error ", round(singular(backend, a); sigdigits = 2))
end

For the smooth integrand, Gauss–Laguerre quadrature converges much faster than the quantile rule, whose error decreases only algebraically.

The logarithm is singular at zero, and its expectation is where Gauss–Laguerre and the quantile rule fall short: both are off by about $10^{-3}$ at 16 points for a shape of 2.5, and at a shape of 0.3, where the mass piles up at zero, Gauss–Laguerre is off by about 1. In the logarithm of $x$ the integrand is smooth, so UnconstrainedLegendre, the rule of EADefault for these families, is accurate to rounding for both integrands at both shapes. Such integrands are the usual ones in inference: the expected logarithm of a rate or a probability, and the log-density of a count or a binary observation. Where a closed form exists, it is still the better choice: a router can put a closed-form backend first, and an integrand that has a closed form is then not integrated numerically.

Both quadratures are more accurate than sampling by orders of magnitude, with far fewer points. Sampling, however, works for any distribution that can be sampled, in any dimension.

Quadrature for families

ExpectationApproximations.GaussHermite — Type
GaussHermite(n; maxpoints = 10_000)

Gauss–Hermite quadrature with n points per dimension, for Gaussian distributions: a Normal, a tensor grid of nᵈ points for a d-dimensional MvNormal, and a LogNormal or LogitNormal through its Gaussian in unconstrained coordinates. The result is exact for polynomials up to degree 2n - 1 of the Gaussian variable; since integrands are not polynomials in general, the backend is not labelled ExpectationExact.

A tensor grid grows exponentially with the dimension, so it has at most maxpoints points. Where nᵈ is larger, the grid has k points per dimension, the largest k with kᵈ ≤ maxpoints, and where that k is below 3, the rule is the 2d points of SphericalRadialCubature. With the default, a grid of 16 points per dimension is kept up to d = 3, and the spherical-radial rule takes over from d = 9.

The points are typed from the distribution's parameters, so dual numbers pass through the parameters as well as the integrand. The points of a Gaussian whose mean is a static vector are static vectors.

source
ExpectationApproximations.UnconstrainedLegendre — Type
UnconstrainedLegendre(n = 8)

Composite Gauss–Legendre quadrature in the unconstrained coordinate of a distribution's domain: u = log x for a positive variable and u = logit x for one on the unit interval (see Domain). It is the rule for Gamma, Exponential, Chisq, Erlang, InverseGamma, Weibull and Beta distributions in EADefault, because the integrands of inference are rarely smooth in x at the boundary: log x, log(1 - x) and the log-density of a Poisson or a Bernoulli observation are singular at 0 or 1, but smooth in u.

The density of u is unimodal for these families. The rule finds its mode, then splits the u axis into panels, with boundaries where the log-density has fallen by 1, 3, 7, 13, 21, 31 and 43 from the mode, and at distances 1, 2, 4, …, 32 from the mode, so that integrands that grow like xᵏ = exp(k u) are resolved too. Each panel has n Gauss–Legendre points, and the weights are the density at the points, normalised to one. The mass beyond the outermost boundaries is below exp(-43). With the default n = 8, at most about 200 points, E[log x] is accurate to about 1e-12 for a Gamma shape or Beta parameter of 0.05 and above, where Gauss–Laguerre and Gauss–Jacobi with 16 points are off by up to 6.

A point whose x would round to the boundary of the domain, such as x < floatmin() for a Gamma with a shape below about 0.04 or 1 - x < eps() for a Beta, is placed at the nearest representable value and keeps its weight. Below those parameters some of the mass is not representable as a Float64 at all, and an integrand singular at that boundary loses accuracy: E[log x] under Gamma(0.01, 1) is off by about 0.08. Near one, floating-point numbers are coarser than near zero: 1 - x at a point close to one carries a relative rounding error of about eps() / (1 - x), so E[log(1 - x)] under a Beta with a second parameter of 0.5 is accurate only to about 1e-8, while E[log x] under one with a first parameter of 0.5 is accurate to rounding.

GaussLaguerre and GaussJacobi remain available, and they are exact for polynomials in x.

prepare_expectation(UnconstrainedLegendre(), q) also works for any other continuous univariate distribution whose log-density is unimodal in the unconstrained coordinate of its domain, the real line when it has none. Support is declared only for the families above. The shapes of a Gamma or a Beta must be plain numbers, while dual numbers pass through the scale of a Gamma and through the integrand.

julia> isapprox(BayesBase.expectation(EA.UnconstrainedLegendre(), log, Exponential(1.0)), -Base.MathConstants.eulergamma; rtol = 1e-12)
true

ExpectationApproximations.GaussLaguerre — Type
GaussLaguerre(n)

Generalised Gauss–Laguerre quadrature with n points for a Gamma or an Exponential distribution: for a shape a, the rule of weight t^(a - 1) exp(-t), so the result is exact for polynomials up to degree 2n - 1 for every shape, including shapes below one. The weights are normalised to one, so very large shapes work too.

The rule depends on the shape, so it is computed when the expectation is prepared, and dual numbers pass through the scale and the integrand but not through the shape.

source
ExpectationApproximations.GaussJacobi — Type
GaussJacobi(n)

Gauss–Jacobi quadrature with n points for a Beta distribution: for shapes a and b, the rule of weight (1 - t)^(b - 1) (1 + t)^(a - 1) mapped onto the unit interval, so the result is exact for polynomials up to degree 2n - 1. The weights are normalised to one.

The rule depends on the shapes, so it is computed when the expectation is prepared, and dual numbers pass through the integrand but not through the shapes.

source
ExpectationApproximations.QuantileLegendre — Type
QuantileLegendre(n)

Gauss–Legendre quadrature with n points in probability space, E_q[f] = ∫₀¹ f(Q(u)) du with Q the quantile function of q, for any continuous univariate distribution. It is a fallback for distributions that no other rule supports. A quantile function is rarely smooth at both ends, so the error decreases algebraically with n, not exponentially. A VonMises distribution and a TransformedGaussian, which have no quantile function, are not supported.

source
ExpectationApproximations.ExactSum — Type
ExactSum()

The exact expectation over a finite support: the sum over all values, weighted by their probabilities. It supports Categorical and other DiscreteNonParametric distributions, Bernoulli, Binomial, DiscreteUniform, Dirac and PointMass, and is labelled ExpectationExact.

source

Sampling

Sampling makes no assumption about smoothness and works in any dimension, but its error decreases only as $1/\sqrt{n}$ and its results are random. Use it for a distribution that no quadrature supports, or as a reference.

ExpectationApproximations.MonteCarlo — Type
MonteCarlo(n, rng = nothing)

The average over n samples, drawn with rng when the expectation is prepared, for any distribution that can be sampled. Labelled ExpectationStochastic. Without a random number generator, preparing an expectation throws an ArgumentError. BayesBase.with_expectation_rng sets a generator, but does not replace one given here.

source
ExpectationApproximations.SNIS — Type
SNIS(proposal, n, rng = nothing)

Self-normalised importance sampling: n samples from proposal, weighted by the ratio of the target density to the proposal density and normalised. It is for a target that can be evaluated but not sampled, such as the unnormalised product of a prior and messages. Labelled ExpectationStochastic. Unless rng is given here, the random number generator comes from BayesBase.with_expectation_rng. The weights are computed in log space, and effective_sample_size estimates how many samples they are worth.

source
ExpectationApproximations.effective_sample_size — Function
effective_sample_size(prepared::ExpectationPointSet)

1 / Σᵢ wᵢ² for normalised weights: the number of equally weighted samples that the weighted ones are worth. It is n when the proposal is the target, and much smaller when the proposal fits the target poorly.

Sigma points

Sigma-point rules use a few deterministic points, chosen to match the first moments of the distribution. They suit smooth functions of Gaussians in several dimensions, as in nonlinear filtering. For a linear function they are exact:

A, b = [1.0 2.0; 0.0 1.0], [0.5, -1.0]
m, V = EA.mean_cov(EA.Unscented(), x -> A * x + b, MvNormal([1.0, 2.0], [1.0 0.3; 0.3 2.0]))
m ≈ A * [1.0, 2.0] + b, V ≈ A * [1.0 0.3; 0.3 2.0] * A'
(true, true)

The centre point of the unscented transform has a covariance weight that differs from its mean weight, so its prepared form is a SigmaPointSet, which stores both. The centre weight can be negative, and it is with the default parameters, so the unscented transform does not support products of distributions or tilted moments. The spherical-radial cubature has equal positive weights and neither restriction.

ExpectationApproximations.Unscented — Type
Unscented(; α = 1e-3, β = 2.0, κ = 0.0)

The unscented transform for Gaussian distributions: 2d + 1 sigma points, the mean and the mean plus and minus the columns of √(d + λ) L, with λ = α²(d + κ) - d and L the Cholesky factor of the covariance. Expectations are exact for polynomials up to degree 3.

The centre has a covariance weight that differs from its mean weight by 1 - α² + β, which mean_cov and mean_cov_cross apply; β = 2 is optimal for Gaussians. With the default α the centre weight is large and negative, so the transform does not support products of distributions, and tilted_mean_cov refuses it.

source
ExpectationApproximations.SigmaPointSet — Type
SigmaPointSet(points, weights, centre)

A prepared expectation over sigma points: a weighted sum with the mean weights weights. The first point, the centre, has a covariance weight that is larger by centre.

BayesBase.prepare_expectation(Unscented(), q) returns a SigmaPointSet instead of a BayesBase.ExpectationPointSet, because the unscented transform weights the centre differently for means and for covariances, and one set of weights cannot express that. BayesBase.expectation uses the mean weights, and mean_cov and mean_cov_cross add the correction.

source
ExpectationApproximations.GenUnscented — Type
GenUnscented()

The generalised unscented transform: three points per dimension, with weights that match the mean, variance, skewness and kurtosis of the distribution, so that expectations are exact for polynomials up to degree 4 of a univariate variable. It supports the univariate families listed below, whose four moments are finite and computed by Distributions, and Gaussian vectors, for which the points lie along the columns of the Cholesky factor of the covariance. For a Gaussian vector the rule matches the moments along each axis, so it is exact for polynomials up to degree 3 and for the fourth powers of single coordinates, but not for products such as x₁² x₂²; for d ≥ 4 its central weight 1 - d / 3 is not positive.

The univariate families are Normal, Gamma, Exponential, Chisq, Erlang, Beta, LogNormal, Weibull, Uniform, Laplace, Logistic, Gumbel, Poisson, Bernoulli, Binomial, Geometric and NegativeBinomial. Others, such as a heavy-tailed distribution with an infinite kurtosis, are not supported, although prepare_expectation can still be called for any univariate distribution whose moments are finite.

source
ExpectationApproximations.SphericalRadialCubature — Type
SphericalRadialCubature()

The third-degree spherical-radial cubature rule for Gaussian distributions: 2d points, the mean plus and minus the columns of √d L, with equal positive weights. It is exact for polynomials up to degree 3.

source

ExponentialFamily's distributions

When ExponentialFamily is loaded, an extension adds support for its distribution types:

  • its univariate Gaussians, NormalMeanVariance, NormalMeanPrecision and NormalWeightedMeanPrecision, to GaussHermite, Unscented, GenUnscented and SphericalRadialCubature;
  • GammaShapeRate to UnconstrainedLegendre and GaussLaguerre.

Its multivariate Gaussians are subtypes of Distributions' AbstractMvNormal, which the package supports without the extension. With the extension, EADefault handles the distributions that the rules of RxInfer produce.
